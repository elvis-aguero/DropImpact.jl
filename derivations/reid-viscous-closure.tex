\section{What is being matched, and why}

Each droplet surface mode in this model is carried by a second-order ODE
(\texttt{eq:lambda-l}; implemented in \texttt{src/affine.jl:drop\_affine})
\begin{equation}
  \ddot\beta_l + 2\lambda_l\dot\beta_l + \omega_l^2\beta_l = F_l\,b_l\text{,}
  \label{eq:mode-ode}
\end{equation}
whose characteristic polynomial is $s^2 + 2\lambda_l s + \omega_l^2$. The published choice
takes Lamb's (1881) small-viscosity asymptotics,
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_l^{\text{Lamb}} = \Oh\,(l-1)(2l+1)\text{,}\qquad
  \big(\omega_l^{\text{Lamb}}\big)^2 = \omega_{l,0}^2 = l(l-1)(l+2)\text{,}
  \label{eq:lamb}
\end{equation}
with $\omega_{l,0}$ the inviscid Rayleigh--Lamb capillary frequency. Reid (1960) instead
gives the \emph{exact} linear viscous eigenvalues at arbitrary $\Oh$, as roots
$\sigma$ of a transcendental characteristic equation (see \texttt{src/reid.jl}); with
$\sigma$ a decay rate, perturbations behave as $e^{-\sigma t}$, so the ODE roots are
$s=-\sigma$.

This note fixes the remaining question: \emph{given a pair of Reid eigenvalues, what are the
faithful $(\lambda_l,\omega_l^2)$?} The algebra is verified symbolically in
\texttt{derivations/cas\_reid\_two\_pole.py} (all assertions pass).

\section{The mapping is Vieta, and needs no case split}

For a monic quadratic with roots $s_1,s_2$,
\begin{equation}
  (s-s_1)(s-s_2) = s^2 + 2\lambda_l s + \omega_l^2
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \boxed{\ \lambda_l = -\tfrac{1}{2}(s_1+s_2)\text{,}\qquad \omega_l^2 = s_1 s_2\ }
  \label{eq:vieta}
\end{equation}
exactly, for \emph{any} pair. Two consequences.

\paragraph{Underdamped ($s = -\gamma \pm i\omega$).}
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_l = \gamma\text{,}\qquad \omega_l^2 = \gamma^2 + \omega^2\text{,}
  \label{eq:case-a}
\end{equation}
\emph{not} $\omega^2$. The distinction matters because \eqref{eq:mode-ode} oscillates at
$\sqrt{\omega_l^2-\lambda_l^2}$, and only \eqref{eq:case-a} returns $\omega$. Lamb
\eqref{eq:lamb} carries the same $O(\lambda^2/\omega^2)$ slip, but there it sits inside its
own asymptotic error; here it would be gratuitous.

\paragraph{Overdamped ($s=-\gamma_1,-\gamma_2$, real, $\gamma_1<\gamma_2$).}
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_l = \tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)\text{,}\qquad
  \omega_l^2 = \gamma_1\gamma_2\text{,}\qquad
  \lambda_l^2-\omega_l^2 = \left(\tfrac{\gamma_1-\gamma_2}{2}\right)^2 \ge 0\text{,}
  \label{eq:case-b}
\end{equation}
so the ODE is genuinely overdamped, degenerating only when the rates coincide. As
$\gamma_2\to\gamma_1$ this reduces continuously to the critically damped case, hence
\eqref{eq:vieta} is a strict generalisation.

\section{The superseded choice, and why it was badly wrong}

An earlier revision of \texttt{drop\_viscous\_coeffs} set, for a real root,
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_l = \gamma_{\text{tracked}}\text{,}\qquad \omega_l^2 = \lambda_l^2\text{,}
  \label{eq:superseded}
\end{equation}
a double root at the tracked rate. This discards $\gamma_2$ and invents a second rate. Two
compounding errors made it worse than a mild approximation:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Wrong branch.} Continuation in $\Oh$ from the complex pair does \emph{not} land
on the slowest real root once the pair has merged. Measured at $l=2$ by scanning the real
axis (the residual is real for real $s>0$, since $q=\sqrt{s/\Oh}$ is then real and so is
$j_l$):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
$\Oh$ & real roots on $(10^{-4},200)$ & tracked & slowest \\
\midrule
$0.8$ & $2.040,\ 3.670,\ 23.94,\ 63.04,\ 118.3,\ 189.4$ & $3.670$ & $2.040$ \\
$1.0$ & $1.280,\ 5.846,\ 29.93,\ 78.80,\ 147.9$ & $5.846$ & $1.280$ \\
$3.0$ & $0.357,\ 20.97,\ 89.86$ & $20.97$ & $0.357$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Continuation consistently returns the \emph{second} root. The slowest root decreases with
$\Oh$ (as a very viscous drop must relax ever more slowly, rate $\propto 1/\Oh$), whereas the
tracked one grows --- the diagnostic that exposed this.

\item \textbf{Wrong stiffness.} Applying \eqref{eq:vieta} to the two slowest roots gives
$\omega_l^2 = 7.487,\ 7.485,\ 7.480$ at $\Oh=0.8,1.0,3.0$, against
$\omega_{2,0}^2 = l(l-1)(l+2) = 8$. The faithful mapping therefore \textbf{preserves the
capillary stiffness}, essentially independent of $\Oh$, as it must: viscosity damps, it does
not change what restores. The superseded choice \eqref{eq:superseded} instead gave
$\omega_l^2=\lambda_l^2 = 20.97^2 \approx 440$ at $\Oh=3$, a stiffness too large by a factor
$59$.
\end{enumerate}

That $\omega_l^2\approx\omega_{l,0}^2$ is not an input to the construction; it emerges, and so
serves as a cheap correctness check on the root pair.

\section{Where the overdamped branch is actually reached}

The capillary pair merges onto the real axis (mode becomes aperiodic) at an $\Oh$ that falls
with $l$: measured between $0.5$ and $0.8$ for $l=2,4$, and between $0.3$ and $0.5$ for
$l=8,16$. So the overdamped case is not hypothetical for $\Oh\gtrsim0.5$, and high modes enter
it first.

\section{Algorithm}

For each $l$, at the requested $\Oh$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Track the least-damped eigenvalue by continuation in $\Oh$ from a value small enough
        that Lamb is genuinely asymptotic (\texttt{reid\_root\_tracked}).
  \item If $|\operatorname{Im}\sigma|>0$: the pair is $\sigma,\bar\sigma$; apply
        \eqref{eq:case-a}.
  \item If $\operatorname{Im}\sigma=0$: the pair has merged. Find the two real roots by a
        \emph{bracketed} search, not a scan --- see \S\ref{sec:bracket} --- and apply
        \eqref{eq:case-b}. Do \emph{not} rely on the continued root being the slowest.
  \item Sanity-check $\omega_l^2$ against $\omega_{l,0}^2$; a large discrepancy indicates a
        bad root pair rather than physics.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Bracketing the dominant pair}
\label{sec:bracket}

The real roots need not be hunted for. In
\begin{equation}
  \text{rhs} = \frac{2(l-1)}{q^2}\left[l + (l+1)\frac{q-2l\,Q}{q-2Q}\right]
  \label{eq:rhs}
\end{equation}
the poles of $Q$ (the zeros of $j_l$) are \textbf{removable}, since
$(q-2lQ)/(q-2Q)\to l$ as $Q\to\infty$. The true singularities are the zeros of
$g(q)=q-2Q(q)$, and the characteristic roots \emph{interlace} with them: measured at $l=2$,
$\Oh=0.8$, the singularities sit at $s=22.56,\,62.72,\,118.13,\,189.26$ and the roots at
$23.94,\,63.04,\,118.31,\,189.37$ --- each root just above its own singularity.

The dominant pair lies below the \emph{first} singularity, so the entire search is confined to
\begin{equation}
  0 < s < s^\ast(l,\Oh) = \Oh\,q^\ast(l)^2\text{,}
  \label{eq:bracket}
\end{equation}
with $q^\ast(l)$ the first zero of $g$. That zero is uniquely bracketed: $g>0$ as $q\to0^+$
(since $Q\sim q/(2l+3)$ gives $g\sim q[1-2/(2l+3)]$), $g\to-\infty$ at the first zero
$jz_1$ of $j_l$, and $g$ has a single maximum between --- so bisection on $(0,jz_1)$ is
rigorous. Crucially $q^\ast$ depends on $l$ \emph{alone}, not on $\Oh$, so it is computed once
and memoised; measured $q^\ast/l = 2.656,\,1.972,\,1.577,\,1.346,\,1.209,\,1.134,\,1.082,\,
1.051$ for $l=2,\dots,240$, always just below $jz_1$.

Verified over $l=2\ldots32$ and $\Oh=0.05\ldots3$ (30 combinations): the interval
\eqref{eq:bracket} contains \textbf{exactly $0$} real roots while the mode oscillates and
\textbf{exactly $2$} once the pair has merged --- never $1$ or $3$. That parity is asserted in
\texttt{test/test\_reid.jl}; an odd count would mean the dominant pair has been
mis-identified and every downstream damping rate is wrong.

A superseded revision instead scanned $40\,000$ log-spaced points over a guessed range. Anchor
$jz_1$ to a bisection of \texttt{besselj} rather than to an asymptotic estimate: a McMahon
first-zero formula overshoots by $0.016$ at $l=2$, which places the bracket on the far side of
the pole and recovers the \emph{second} singularity ($8.854$) in place of the first ($5.311$).

\section{Evaluating the Bessel ratio, and whether the seed truncation is justified}

The characteristic equation needs only the ratio $Q_l(q)=j_{l+1}(q)/j_l(q)$, never $j_l$
itself. Forming $j_l$ is in fact unsafe: \texttt{besselj} \emph{overflows} at small $\Oh$,
where $|q|\sim50$ with a large imaginary part, and \emph{underflows} at large order with small
argument. The latter is not hypothetical --- during development it silently returned
$q^\ast(120)=0.271$ against a true $129.86$, precisely the class of failure that yields a
plausible-looking wrong answer rather than an error.

We therefore use the continued fraction in recurrence form. From
$j_{n-1}+j_{n+1}=\tfrac{2n+1}{q}j_n$, dividing by $j_n$,
\begin{equation}
  Q_{n-1} = \frac{1}{\dfrac{2n+1}{q} - Q_n}\text{,}\qquad
  Q_{n_0}\;\simeq\;\frac{q}{2n_0+3}\text{,}
  \label{eq:cf}
\end{equation}
recursed downward from $n_0$ to $l$.

\paragraph{The seed is \emph{not} justified by being accurate.} Expanding the exact ratio
(verified in \texttt{derivations/cas\_bessel\_ratio\_seed.py}),
\begin{equation}
  Q_n = \frac{q}{2n+3}\left[1 + \frac{q^2}{4n^2+16n+15} + O(q^4)\right]\text{,}
  \label{eq:seed-remainder}
\end{equation}
so the seed's \emph{relative} remainder is $O(q^2/n^2)$, which is $O(1)$ --- not small ---
whenever $n_0$ is merely comparable to $|q|$. Taking \eqref{eq:cf}'s seed on its own would be
an unjustified truncation.

\paragraph{It is justified because the recurrence contracts.} Differentiating \eqref{eq:cf},
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial Q_{n-1}}{\partial Q_n} = Q_{n-1}^2
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  e_{n-1} = Q_{n-1}^2\,e_n
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  E_{n-1} = Q_{n-1}Q_n\,E_n
  \label{eq:contraction}
\end{equation}
for absolute error $e$ and relative error $E=e/Q$. With $Q_n\sim q/(2n+3)$ the per-step
relative factor is $q^2/[(2n+1)(2n+3)]\sim(q/2n)^2\ll1$ as soon as $2n\gg|q|$, and the
cumulative factor from $n_0$ down to $l+1$ is a product of such terms --- geometric, hence
accuracy exponential in the padding. This is the Miller premise.

\paragraph{Consequence for the testbed.} Since the justification is contraction rather than
seed accuracy, the property to assert in the source is \textbf{pad-independence}, not
agreement of the seed with anything. Measured relative error against a scaled-Bessel reference:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llccccc}
\toprule
$l$ & $q$ & pad $0$ & $2$ & $8$ & $30$ & $120$ \\
\midrule
$2$ & $5$ & $2.7\!\times\!10^{-4}$ & $1.1\!\times\!10^{-6}$ & $1.6\!\times\!10^{-15}$ & $1.2\!\times\!10^{-16}$ & $1.2\!\times\!10^{-16}$ \\
$2$ & $37.6+37.6i$ & $1.2\!\times\!10^{-16}$ & \multicolumn{4}{c}{(flat)} \\
$16$ & $21.5$ & $4.8\!\times\!10^{-14}$ & $1.1\!\times\!10^{-15}$ & \multicolumn{3}{c}{(flat)} \\

\end{tabular}
\end{center}

There is a genuinely failing regime at small padding, so the test discriminates. The shipped
choice $n_0 = l + \max(60,\,l/2+|q|) + |q|$ sits in the plateau at every point checked
($l$ up to $240$), with doubling the padding changing no digit.

\section{Scope}

Two limitations are inherited and unchanged by this note. First, \eqref{eq:mode-ode} is a
two-pole model: Reid's spectrum also contains an infinite diffusive ladder (internal Stokes
relaxations, rates $\approx\Oh\,q_j^2$ with $q_j$ the zeros of $j_l$), so the \emph{forced}
response carries memory that no second-order ODE reproduces. Matching two poles exactly is
right for free decay and approximate under forcing; the $n$-pole generalisation is deferred.
Second, the residues are not used here --- only the poles --- so this note says nothing about
the amplitude with which pressure forcing excites each mode.
